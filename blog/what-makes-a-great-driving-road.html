The thesis

What makes a great driving road

Everyone knows one when they are on it. Almost nobody can say what it is. I spent a year trying to write it down, and the feeling turns out to come apart into things you can count.

Neil Bamford · 12 August 2026 · 8 min read

There is a moment about four minutes into a good road where you stop thinking about it. You have read enough corners to know what the next one is going to do. Your hands have found the rhythm. You are not driving carefully any more, you are just driving, and the road and the car and you have quietly agreed on something.

That is the thing. That is what people are actually chasing when they get up at six on a Sunday and drive two hours to somewhere they have already been. Not speed particularly, and not scenery particularly. That feeling of a road that lets you in.

The problem is that nobody can describe it. Ask ten drivers what makes a great road and you will get ten answers that are all correct and none of which help. Flow. Sight lines. Character. It just works. Everybody is pointing at the same thing from a different angle and nobody can hand you the thing itself.

Which is why every list of Britain's best driving roads is really a list of the roads that particular writer happened to be sent to, remembered fondly, and could get a photographer to. That is not a criticism. It is just what happens when the criterion is a feeling.

So we tried something else. Instead of trying to define a great road, we drove them and wrote down what we actually felt, sentence by sentence, and then worked backwards to ask what was physically happening on the road when we felt it. That is the entire idea behind Cambr. It turns out the feeling is not mystical at all. It comes apart into about eight things, and every one of them is something you can measure across an entire country.

The corners have to arrive in a rhythm

Not just lots of corners. Anyone can find lots of corners, and a road that turns constantly with no pattern to it is exhausting rather than good. What you are actually feeling is cadence: corners arriving at a spacing your hands can settle into, so that the exit of one is already setting up the entry to the next.

This is the most obvious thing to count and the easiest one to get wrong. A road that averages a corner every two hundred metres might be beautifully even the whole way, or it might be twenty bends stacked into one mile and then five miles of nothing. Those are completely different roads to drive and they produce identical averages. How much a road turns matters. Whether it keeps doing it matters just as much.

You have to be able to see

This is the one people underrate most, and it is probably the single biggest divider between a road you enjoy and a road you endure.

Commitment is a function of visibility. On a road where you can see through the corner you will carry pace naturally and never feel like you are taking a risk. On a road with the same corners and a hedge on the apex you will be on the brakes the whole way, and it will feel like work. The corners are identical. The experience is not.

Some of that is about how wide the road is and what kind of road it is. Some of it is about what is beside it, because a road across open moorland shows you the next half mile and a road between banked hedgerows shows you the next forty feet. Both are countable, and together they explain an enormous amount about why one road feels generous and another feels mean.

It has to go up and down

A flat road can be good. A road that climbs and drops is almost always better, and I do not think this gets said enough.

Partly it is the physical sensation, the compression at the bottom of a dip and the lightness over a crest. Partly it is that elevation changes what you can see, so the road keeps revealing itself instead of laying out flat in front of you. And partly it is that hills put the car under load in a way that flat corners do not, so you feel more of what it is doing.

The best roads in Britain are almost all mountain or moorland crossings, and this is why. Not the views. The gradient.

You have to trust the surface

Surface is the thing that decides whether you can use everything else. A road can have perfect corners, perfect sight lines and perfect elevation, and if the tarmac is patched and scarred and you cannot predict what the front axle is going to do, you will back off exactly where the road is asking you to commit.

It is also the least romantic thing on this list and the one nobody puts in an article, because nobody photographs tarmac. But ask anyone who has driven the same road before and after it was resurfaced.

You have to have it to yourself

This is the killer, and it is the reason so many famous roads disappoint people who drive them for the first time on a Bank Holiday.

A great road with a motorhome on it is not a great road. It is a queue with a nice view. Everything above depends on being able to use the road, and being able to use the road depends on there being space to use it in.

What is interesting is that this is measurable in a way that surprised us. The speed limit on a road tells you almost nothing, because a rural lane is legally derestricted whether or not you could ever do sixty on it. What tells you something is how fast traffic actually moves along it in practice. That number quietly encodes width, traffic, junctions, surface and how many tourists stop halfway up to take a picture, all at once.

It also cuts both ways, which took us a while to accept. Some of the best roads in Britain are slow. That is a different article.

Nobody watching

This is not about wanting to speed. It is about mental load.

A road with a camera every mile changes how you drive even when you are nowhere near the limit, because part of your attention is permanently on the speedometer instead of on the road. You cannot settle. The road might be superb and you will still spend the whole of it half-distracted.

Some genuinely great roads are heavily enforced, usually for reasons that are entirely justified by their casualty records. That does not make them worse roads. It does change what driving them is like, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

Somewhere worth being

Roads do not exist in isolation. The A82 through Glencoe would be a good road anywhere. Through Glencoe it is something else, and anybody who says landscape does not matter has not driven it.

The trick is not letting this dominate, because scenery is exactly what makes people overrate roads. It should be the thing that lifts a good road into a memorable one, not the thing that gets a mediocre road onto a list.

Enough of it to get into it

Back to the four minutes. There is a length below which a road cannot be great, no matter how good it is, because you do not have time to stop thinking about it.

A mile of perfect road is a nice mile. It is not a drive. You arrive at the good bit already braking for the end of it, and you never reach the point where the road lets you in. Some of the most photographed stretches in Britain are barely a mile long, and it is the main reason they do not rate as highly as people expect.

What we cannot measure

Plenty, and it is worth saying so.

We cannot measure whether you were in the right car, and the right car changes everything. We cannot measure the light, and a road at seven in the morning in October is a different road to the same tarmac at two in the afternoon in July. We cannot measure whether you had somewhere to be, or who was in the passenger seat, or whether you had been looking forward to it for a month.

Those things are most of why anybody remembers a drive. They are also completely personal, which is precisely why they are not the job. What we can do is tell you, before you set off, which roads are worth pointing the car at. What happens after that is yours.

And the thing it still gets wrong

One, specifically, and I would rather say it than have someone find it.

Britain is covered in narrow single-track farm lanes that are twisty, hilly, quiet and derestricted. On paper that is a driving road. In reality it is four feet narrower than your car needs, has grass down the middle and a tractor coming the other way. Telling those apart from a genuinely great narrow mountain pass is a harder data problem than it sounds, because the national mapping data we have models both of them as roughly the same width.

We have got a long way with it and we are not finished. If the map sends you down something that turns out to be somebody's driveway, tell us and we will take it off. That feedback loop is a real part of how this gets better, not a line in a press release.

Why bother

Because the alternative is what we have now, which is that the same forty roads get written about repeatedly and everything else stays invisible. Measuring all of them rather than the shortlist is the only way to find the ones nobody was sent to.

The best thing that has come out of doing it this way is not the ranking of the famous roads, which mostly confirms what the motoring press already worked out over thirty years. It is the empty B-road in County Durham that nobody has ever written about and that turns out to be one of the best roads in England.

That is the bit no list of somebody's favourites can ever give you.
