A finding

Why Britain's most photographed roads are not its best drives

Winnats Pass is 1.4 km long and traffic moves through it at about 22 mph. We measured every road in Britain, and the ones that photograph best are consistently not the ones that drive best.

Neil Bamford · 12 August 2026 · 7 min read

You have seen Winnats Pass. Even if you have never been to the Peak District you have seen it, because it is on the front of half the walking guides in Britain and it turns up on your phone about once a fortnight. A narrow ribbon of tarmac dropping between two enormous green limestone shoulders, usually shot in low autumn light with a single car on it for scale.

It is one of the most photographed roads in the country. When we measured it, it came out in our bottom tier.

That is not a provocation and I am not interested in telling anybody their favourite road is rubbish. It is a result, and the reason it happens is genuinely interesting, because it says something about the difference between looking at a road and driving one.

The two numbers that explain it

Winnats Pass is 1.4 kilometres long. Traffic moves along it at an average of about 22 mph.

That is most of the answer right there. Under a mile is not a drive. You arrive at the good bit already braking for the end of it, and you never get to the point where you settle into a road and stop thinking about it. And 22 mph on a derestricted road is not a limit doing that, it is the road itself: narrow, steep, permanently full of people who have stopped to take exactly the photograph you have seen.

Add to that a road that does not actually turn very much. It looks like it should, because the walls on either side are dramatic and the gradient is severe. But the tarmac itself runs fairly straight down the middle of the cleft. The drama is the landscape, not the road.

The drama is the landscape, not the road. A photograph cannot tell those apart. Driving it can.

What a photograph rewards

Think about what makes a road photogenic and it becomes obvious that it is close to unrelated to what makes one good to drive.

A photograph rewards a shape you can see from outside the road: a switchback stack you can frame from a hillside, a ribbon threading through something vast, a strong contrast between tarmac and terrain. It rewards being narrow, because a wide road looks like infrastructure. It rewards being short, because you need the whole thing in one frame. It rewards being somewhere with a good vantage point, which usually means somewhere with a car park and a footpath, which usually means somewhere busy.

Driving rewards the opposite of almost all of that. It rewards what happens inside the road, over time: corners in a rhythm, sight lines you can commit to, elevation under load, and enough length to get into it. None of those photograph at all. Nobody has ever taken a picture of good cadence.

The same pattern, repeatedly

Hardknott and Wrynose. Probably the most shared pair of roads in England, and justifiably so, because a photograph of the Hardknott hairpins looks like something out of the Alps. Traffic gets through Hardknott at under 16 mph. It is three kilometres long. It is single-track with passing places on a 1-in-3 gradient. It is a magnificent thing to have done and it is not, by any sensible reading, a great drive.

Bealach na Bà. The most Alpine road in Britain and one I would recommend to anybody. It sits a tier below the top on our map, at about 22 mph, and the reason is the same: it is a single-track climb where most of the effort is managing oncoming traffic rather than driving. Being a bucket-list road and being a great driving road are different claims and both are true of it.

The Rest and Be Thankful. A famous name, a genuinely spectacular view down Glen Croe, and years of landslides and diversions. It photographs like a headline. It drives like an A-road.

Cheddar Gorge. One of the great natural set-pieces in England and theatrical from the first corner. It also rates well below its fame, because the bottom section is permanently full of people at walking pace and the road is only properly good once you are out of the gorge and onto the Mendip tops, which is the bit nobody photographs.

The counter-example that matters

Here is where the easy version of this argument falls over, and I think it is the most interesting thing in the whole exercise.

If slow roads simply scored badly, this would be a boring finding about speed. But the B3306 along Cornwall's west coast between St Ives and St Just moves at about 23 mph, which is slower than Winnats, and it comes out in our top tier. It is one of the highest-rated roads in Britain.

So speed is not the discriminator. What separates them is that the B3306 is twenty-two kilometres rather than one and a half, and it turns constantly for all of them. It is slow because it is relentlessly technical, not because it is congested. You get the four minutes. You get an hour, in fact, and by the end of it you are driving a completely different way than you were at the start.

Winnats is over before anything has begun. That is the difference, and it has nothing to do with how fast either of them lets you go.

What photographs like nothing

The inverse is the part that made this project worth doing.

Three of the highest-rated roads in England are B-roads across the North Pennines, running between Consett, Middleton-in-Teesdale, Brough and Allenheads. One of them is 47 kilometres of wide, fast, well-surfaced moorland road where you can drive for half an hour and meet nothing.

Nobody photographs them. There is no vantage point, no hairpin stack, no cleft in a limestone hillside. It is just a very good road going across a lot of open ground, which is completely unphotographable and absolutely brilliant to drive.

They also barely appear in driving coverage, and I do not think that is a coincidence. Roads get written about partly because there is a picture in it. A stretch of Weardale that looks like every other stretch of Weardale never gets the commission, no matter what it is like at seven in the morning with nothing else on it.

None of this makes them bad roads

Worth being clear, because this sort of thing gets read as a takedown and it is not one.

Winnats is one of the most striking places in England and you should absolutely drive it. So should you drive Hardknott, once, in something small. Bealach na Bà is a genuine event. The point is not that these roads are overrated. It is that "spectacular" and "great to drive" are two different properties, they get conflated constantly, and if you plan a weekend using only the first one you will spend it in traffic.

The useful version is knowing both. Drive Winnats because it is Winnats, and then drive the twenty miles of the Peak District that nobody photographs on your way home.

We might be wrong

Genuinely. These are measurements, not verdicts, and measurements can be badly calibrated. If enough people who know Winnats well tell us it deserves better than it got, that is evidence and we will go and look at what we are counting.

That would be a better article than this one, not a worse one. But we would want to be told rather than assume, which is why every road on the map can be argued with.

If you want the reasoning behind what actually gets counted, it is in what makes a great driving road.
