The most underrated driving region in England

Six of Britain's twenty-three top-tier roads sit between Skipton and Hexham. Three of them are B-roads that almost nobody writes about, and they are among the best driving in the country.

Neil Bamford · 12 August 2026 · 10 min read

If you ask where the best driving in England is, most people say the Lake District or the Peak District. Both are excellent and both are wrong, at least in terms of concentration. The densest run of genuinely great road in England is the band of moorland that runs from the Yorkshire Dales up through Teesdale and Weardale into the North Pennines, and a good part of it barely appears in driving coverage at all.

Six roads in this band have reached the top of our scale. Three of them are the ones you would expect. The other three are B-roads that turned up when we measured every road in Britain rather than the ones people already write about, and finding them is the single best argument for measuring things.

The North Pennines has three top-tier roads in it. Between them they get a fraction of the coverage Winnats Pass gets for being photogenic.

The Dales

  1. S-tierHawes to Thwaite10 km

    Buttertubs Pass

    The best-known road in the Dales and the one that earns it. A sharp crossing between Wensleydale and Swaledale, all sudden climbs, blind crests and tight technical work between high fells, named after the limestone shafts near the summit. Ten kilometres is short, but there is essentially nothing wasted in it.

    Long regarded across the motoring press as one of the great Yorkshire driving roads.

  2. S-tierB6255 · Ingleton to Hawes27 km

    B6255 Ribblehead

    The counterpoint to Buttertubs, and the case for a completely different kind of good road. Wide, fast and open, past the Ribblehead viaduct through Three Peaks country, with big sweeping bends instead of tight ones and views that go on for miles. It averages around 38 mph, which for a national-limit B-road across moorland is about as good as it gets.

  3. A-tierA684 · Hawes to Sedbergh40 km

    A684

    Runs west from Hawes towards the Howgills, wide and well-sighted with a steady supply of fast sweepers. Not a road that demands much of you, which is exactly why it works as the connective tissue of a Dales day.

  4. A-tierA683 · Kirkby Stephen to Sedbergh19 km

    A683 Lune valley

    Sweeping down the Lune valley beneath the Howgill Fells. Open, quick and with a genuinely lovely natural rhythm to it. Pairs with the A684 into an obvious loop.

  5. A-tierB6270 · Kirkby Stephen to Richmond33 km

    B6270 Swaledale

    The full length of Swaledale, following the river through stone-walled villages with a constant rhythm of bends. Less a household name than a local's road, and it is the natural approach or exit for Buttertubs from the north.

  6. A-tierB6160 · Hawes to Kettlewell15 km

    Fleet Moss

    The highest road in the Yorkshire Dales, a steep and lonely climb between Wensleydale and Wharfedale. Narrow and exposed near the top, and prized more for the achievement and the view than for outright pace. Cyclists rate it for the same reasons and there will be plenty of them.

The North Pennines

Cross north from the Dales and the roads get emptier, wider and faster. This is the least-driven quarter of upland England, and it holds three top-tier roads.

  1. S-tierA686 · Penrith to Alston37 km

    A686 Hartside Pass

    The famous one. The corners come thick and fast on the climb out of the Eden valley, building through blind, tightening bends to a summit with views clear across to the Lakes. A fixture of best-driving-road features for decades. The summit café burned down in 2018, which changed the ritual but not the road.

  2. S-tierB6278 · Consett to Middleton-in-Teesdale47 km

    B6278

    Forty-seven kilometres of fast, wide, well-surfaced B-road striking across the high North Pennines. Sweeping moorland miles, big elevation swings, and long stretches where you will not see another vehicle. It is the longest road in our top tier and one of the least covered anywhere in the motoring press.

  3. S-tierB6295 · Cowshill to Allenheads25 km

    B6295 Weardale to Allendale

    Links Weardale to Allendale over the tops, and it is the road that most surprised us when the numbers came out. High, empty and consistently engaging, in a part of England where you can drive for half an hour without meeting anything.

  4. S-tierB6276 · Middleton-in-Teesdale to Brough22 km

    B6276

    A high crossing from Teesdale over to the Eden valley at Brough, and the third of the three Pennines B-roads. Take it with the B6278 and the B6295 and you have close to a hundred kilometres of top-tier driving in a region most people pass through on the A66.

  5. A-tierB6277 · Alston to Barnard Castle24 km

    B6277

    Wide, fast and empty between Alston and Barnard Castle, with well-sighted sweepers and very little to interrupt them.

  6. A-tierB6277 spur to St John's Chapel6 km

    Harthope Moss

    A short, sharp moorland climb rising fast over open fell at the national limit. Six kilometres, and worth the detour off the B6277 for what it does with them. This is the highest classified road in England.

The North York Moors, an hour east

  1. S-tierHutton-le-Hole to Castleton9 km

    Blakey Ridge

    Riding the spine of the North York Moors, high and open, flowing fast and uninterrupted with sight lines that let you read every bend well ahead. The Lion Inn at the top is one of the great British driving stops. Nine kilometres, and it makes the top tier on quality per mile alone.

  2. A-tierB1257 · Stokesley to Helmsley38 km

    B1257 Clay Bank

    A genuinely entertaining run across the moors over the Clay Bank pass, with a long sequence of sharp and sweeping corners and twenty-odd miles of moorland to enjoy them in. The obvious pairing with Blakey Ridge.

A two-day route that takes in most of it

Day one: the Dales

Start at Ingleton. B6255 east past Ribblehead to Hawes. Fleet Moss south and back if the weather is good, then Buttertubs north over into Swaledale, and the B6270 east down the dale. Return west on the A684 and drop down the A683 through the Lune valley. Base at Hawes or Kirkby Stephen.

Day two: the Pennines

North from Kirkby Stephen over the B6276 to Middleton-in-Teesdale. The B6278 north-east all the way to Consett is the long, empty centrepiece. Come back south-west on the B6295 through Allenheads to Cowshill, then the B6277 down to Alston and the A686 over Hartside to finish in the Eden valley.

That is six top-tier roads across two days, with a total of perhaps four hundred miles. Add the North York Moors as a third day if you have it, or as a separate trip from Teesside.

Practical notes. These are high moorland roads and they close in snow, Hartside and the B6278 among the first. Sheep are unfenced across most of the Pennines section. Fuel is sparse between Middleton-in-Teesdale and Alston, so fill up before the Pennines day rather than during it. And the Lion Inn on Blakey Ridge is worth planning the day around.

Why these roads are not on more lists

Driving coverage tends to follow driving coverage. A road gets written about, so the next writer goes there, so it gets written about again. It is a reasonable system and it has produced a lot of good journalism, but it has a blind spot: roads that were never written about the first time stay unwritten about, no matter how good they are.

Measuring every road rather than the shortlist is the only way past that. The three North Pennines B-roads are what came out of it here. There will be more elsewhere.
